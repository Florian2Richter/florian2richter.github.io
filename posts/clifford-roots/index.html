How finely can you divide a depolarizing channel?

quantum
channels
clifford
finite-fields
Clifford channels are a tiny corner of the space of quantum channels, and yet they let you divide the most destructive channel of all into nearly as many discrete steps as the full quantum machinery allows. The room to do this lives in a surprising place.
Author

Florian Richter

Published

May 30, 2026

Take a photograph and make a photocopy. Then a photocopy of the photocopy. Do it again. And again.

If you keep going long enough, the picture is gone. What’s on the page is grey noise that could plausibly have started as almost anything. The information about the original has been erased.

This is a familiar form of loss. Each pass through the copier loses a little, the losses pile up, and eventually nothing remains. Heat works this way too. So does mixing dye into water, or scrambling a Rubik’s cube, or letting milk cool in a glass. Everywhere you look, processes that destroy information are processes that destroy it in steps: many small irreversible nudges that add up.

Now think about how you’d design such a process. You have full control of the photocopier. The cheap thing you can do is make it violently destructive: a single pass turns any input into grey noise. Erasure in one step. Done.

You can also build copiers that never quite finish in any finite number of passes, and only erase everything in the infinite limit of repeated copying. This is a genuinely interesting region of design space. Characterising which copiers really do erase everything in the limit (as opposed to settling onto some non-trivial steady image, or oscillating, or simply slowing to a crawl) is the subject of a substantial literature on ergodic and mixing channels.1 It is not, however, the question of this post.

This post’s question sits between those two. How slowly can you erase, while still finishing in finitely many steps?

Concretely: among all copy-of-a-copy operations that erase the original after some exact number k of identical applications, how large can k be?

That largest k is where this post lives, and it has different answers in different settings. If the copier has to behave like a classical probabilistic process, you get one bound. Upgrade to quantum mechanics, and the bound becomes quadratically larger. Then restrict the quantum copier to a tightly constrained, low-parameter family of operations, a family with vastly less freedom than the general quantum case, and you might expect the bound to drop most of the way back down.

It almost doesn’t. The factor-of-2 gap that remains is the punchline. The reason it remains is a piece of finite-field bookkeeping that turns out, if you sit with it, to be unreasonably beautiful.

The classical setup

Let’s make the photocopier mathematically precise. We start with the classical case, where everything is ordinary probability theory.

Pick a small number of distinct states that your image can be in. Say there are three: black, grey, and white. Then “the image” at any moment is described by a probability distribution over these three states. Maybe right now the image is definitely white, which we write as (0, 0, 1). Maybe after some processing it has drifted to (0.4, 0.5, 0.1): 40% likely black, 50% grey, 10% white, the uncertainty standing in for whatever noise has crept in.

The set of all such probability distributions over d states is a familiar geometric object: a (d - 1)-dimensional simplex (Figure 1). For d = 3 it is a triangle. The corners are the pure states (definitely this colour, no uncertainty), and the centre is the uniform distribution (totally unsure, every colour equally likely).

A copy operation takes one probability distribution as input and returns another. Before we write down what kind of object such an operation is, it is worth asking what we want to insist on.

The first thing is linearity. The copier S has to be a linear map: if you feed it a coin-flip mixture of two inputs, the output has to be the same coin-flip mixture of the two separate outputs. Anything else would let the copier react to information beyond what the input distribution actually contains, which is a contradiction in terms.

Why must mixtures behave this way?

Suppose you decide your input on the flip of a coin: with probability \alpha you start from distribution p, and with probability 1 - \alpha you start from q. The total input, before you flip the coin, is the mixture \alpha p + (1 - \alpha) q.

Apply the copier S. You can describe what comes out in two ways. From inside the experiment, the output is “S(p) with probability \alpha, S(q) otherwise,” which is the distribution \alpha S(p) + (1 - \alpha) S(q). From outside, ignoring the coin flip, you simply fed the copier a single mixture, so the output is S(\alpha p + (1 - \alpha) q).

These two descriptions had better agree: S\bigl(\alpha p + (1 - \alpha) q\bigr) \;=\; \alpha S(p) + (1 - \alpha) S(q). If they disagreed, “the input is a probability distribution” would stop meaning what we want it to mean. So S is linear.

And a linear map is just a matrix. So from here on our copier is a matrix, which we will call S, and applying it to a distribution is ordinary matrix multiplication.

That already pins down what the entries of S can look like. Feed in a pure state, all the probability on one entry, and the output is just one column of S. For that column to be a probability distribution in its own right, its entries must be non-negative and sum to one: S_{ij} \geq 0, \qquad \sum_i S_{ij} = 1. The same holds for every column, and linearity then takes care of every mixture in between. A matrix of this kind, non-negative entries with every column summing to one, is called a stochastic matrix: exactly the linear maps that send probability distributions to probability distributions.

There is one more restriction we will impose, and it turns out to be the most interesting one in retrospect. We will only consider copiers that leave the uniform distribution untouched: the flat distribution that puts equal weight on every state has to come back out exactly as it went in.

Why? The uniform distribution represents no information about the state of the image: every outcome equally likely. A copier that maps “no information” to “some information” would be generating knowledge out of nothing, which is the opposite of what an erasure process ought to do. For a process whose job is to lose information, the uniform distribution is a natural fixed point to leave alone. (In the quantum upgrade later in the post, the analogous restriction is more than just natural, it is the standard convention. For now, take it as a sensible thing to ask for.)

This small demand has a tidy consequence. We already know the columns of S sum to one. Insisting that the uniform distribution survives forces the rows to sum to one as well. A stochastic matrix with that extra symmetry is called bistochastic, and from here on “copier” will mean “bistochastic matrix.”

The one-line computation

Write u = (1/d, \ldots, 1/d) for the uniform distribution and ask for Su = u. Reading off entry i of the output, (Su)_i \;=\; \sum_j S_{ij} \cdot \frac{1}{d} \;=\; \frac{1}{d} \sum_j S_{ij} \;\overset{!}{=}\; \frac{1}{d}, which is exactly \sum_j S_{ij} = 1 for every row i.

One small fact worth pinning down before we go further: the product of two bistochastic matrices is bistochastic, which you can verify directly from the row and column sum conditions. So S^k stays bistochastic for any k, and the question of finite roots is going to be a question about powers of matrices that stay safely inside our chosen class.

Now we can say precisely what “total erasure” means. The most depolarising copier you can write down is the one whose every column is the uniform distribution. Every input state, no matter which one, produces the same uniform output: P = \frac{1}{d}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & \cdots & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & \cdots & 1 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 1 & 1 & \cdots & 1 \end{pmatrix}.

Apply P to anything and you get the uniform distribution back. All information about the input has been thrown away in a single step. Apply P again and nothing changes: the simplex has been crushed to a single point, the centre, and P just keeps mapping it there. This P is bistochastic by inspection (every row sums to one as well), and it is the completely depolarising channel in dimension d, or CDC for short.

With P pinned down we can ask the post’s question, precisely. A k-th root of P is a bistochastic matrix S such that S^k = P \quad \text{and} \quad S^r \ne P \text{ for any } r < k. The second condition is just “the erasure really happens at step k, not earlier.” How large can k be?

The answer is sharp: k \leq d - 1.

The reason is structural. The map P has rank one: it crushes the entire (d - 1)-dimensional space of traceless deviations from the uniform distribution down to zero. For S^k to do that, S itself must carry a nilpotent part on that traceless space, and a nilpotent operator on a (d - 1)-dimensional space takes at most d - 1 steps to reach zero. That is the bound.

The Jordan-block argument

Two facts make the powers of S easy to control. First, P is idempotent, P^2 = P. Second, P absorbs S from either side: SP = P because S fixes the uniform distribution, and PS = P because the columns of S sum to one.

Now peel off the part of S that is not already P. Set N = S - P. The two absorption facts give PN = NP = 0, so P and N never interfere, and the binomial expansion collapses to S^k = (P + N)^k = P + N^k. So S^k = P exactly when N^k = 0. And N is nilpotent on the right space: it kills the uniform direction (Nu = Su - Pu = u - u = 0) and sends everything else into the (d-1)-dimensional space of traceless vectors.

A nilpotent operator is governed by its Jordan form, a stack of blocks each of which powers to zero in exactly its own size. The largest one that fits in d - 1 dimensions is the single (d-1) \times (d-1) block with ones on the superdiagonal, N \;\cong\; \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & & & \\ & 0 & 1 & & \\ & & \ddots & \ddots & \\ & & & 0 & 1 \\ & & & & 0 \end{pmatrix}. Each power pushes that diagonal of ones one step toward the corner, so N^{d-1} = 0 while N^{d-2} \neq 0. The smallest k with N^k = 0 is therefore at most d - 1, which is the bound. It is tight: this very block, as long as P + N stays bistochastic, gives a root of order exactly d - 1.

For d = 2 the bound says k = 1, which is to say there are no non-trivial roots. The two-state CDC can only be split into one step, itself. Total erasure of one classical bit is a one-shot affair.

For d = 3 the bound is k = 2. A square root of the bistochastic 3 \times 3 CDC exists, and it acts on the simplex by funnelling every point along a two-step trajectory to the centre (Figure 2).

And that is the classical story. A clean bound, a clean construction, and not much more to say about it.

The interesting thing is what happens to that bound when we let ourselves leave classical probability behind.

The quantum upgrade

If qubits and the |0\rangle, |1\rangle notation aren’t already part of your mental furniture, the gentlest on-ramp I know of is Matuschak and Nielsen’s Quantum Computing for the Very Curious. Read it first, or just keep going and chase the link when something looks unfamiliar. We will lean on it rather than rebuild it.

Everything so far was a rehearsal. The real question is about quantum information, and a quantum state carries more than a probability distribution does. More in a way we can count, and the count turns out to be the whole plot of this section.

Go back to the smallest example. A classical bit is a probability distribution over two outcomes: one number between zero and one, with the rest left over. Those states form a line segment. One dimension of freedom, the two definite bits at its ends and the coin-flip state in the middle.

A quantum bit, a qubit, has more room than that line. Its state is no longer a list of probabilities but a small matrix, called a density matrix. You do not need its inner workings here, only its shape: the states of a single qubit fill a solid ball in three dimensions, the Bloch ball (Figure 3). The definite states, the quantum versions of “this bit is certainly 0,” sit on the surface. Blends of them sit inside. At the dead centre is the state of total ignorance, the quantum coin flip.

Notice what happened to the dimension. The classical bit lived on a line, one dimension. The qubit lives in a ball, three. That is no accident, and the pattern is the point. A quantum state in dimension d is a d \times d density matrix: Hermitian, with non-negative eigenvalues, and trace one. Count the real numbers it takes to pin one down and you get d^2 - 1.

Where $d^2 - 1$ comes from

A d \times d Hermitian matrix is fixed by d^2 real numbers: d real numbers on the diagonal, plus the entries above the diagonal, each complex and so worth two reals, with everything below the diagonal forced by Hermiticity. The off-diagonal count is 2 \cdot \tfrac{d(d-1)}{2} = d(d-1), and d + d(d-1) = d^2. Fixing the trace to one removes a single degree of freedom, leaving d^2 - 1.

So the quantum state space is (d^2 - 1)-dimensional, against the classical simplex’s d - 1. For a single bit, three against one. The freedom grew quadratically, and every result in this post is a shadow of that one fact.

The rest of the cast carries over almost unchanged. A quantum copier is a channel: a linear map that turns density matrices into density matrices. It has to be linear for exactly the reason it was classically, that a coin-flip mixture of inputs must come out as the same coin-flip mixture of outputs. There is one extra demand with no classical echo, called complete positivity, which insists the map keeps sending valid states to valid states even when it acts on only part of a larger entangled system. Channels are well understood objects with a clean structure theory.2 For us they are simply the right notion of “physically allowed operation,” and we will not pry them open.

We make the same restriction we made classically. Out of all channels we keep the ones that leave the state of total ignorance untouched. Classically that was the uniform distribution. Here it is the maximally mixed state, the centre of the Bloch ball, written I/d with I the identity matrix. A channel that fixes it invents no information from nothing and secretly prefers no state to any other, which is the least we should ask of a process whose only job is to forget. (Such channels have a name, unital, but the name matters less than the picture.)

Total erasure is the same idea as before, too. The completely depolarising channel sends every input, no exceptions, to the maximally mixed state. The whole Bloch ball collapses to its centre point in a single step. This is the quantum CDC, and it is the operator we now want to take roots of.

Here is where that quadratic shows up where it counts. Ask the same question as in the classical case: how large can k be, if a channel S satisfies S^k = P but no smaller power does? The argument has the same skeleton. The quantum CDC is again a rank-one map: it crushes the entire space of traceless operators, the directions away from the maximally mixed state, down to zero. Any S powering to it must carry a nilpotent part on that space, and a single nilpotent block on a space of dimension D takes at most D steps to wind down. Only now D = d^2 - 1 rather than d - 1, so k \;\leq\; d^2 - 1.

Set d = 2. The classical bound was k \leq 1: a single bit could not be erased in more than one step. The quantum bound is k \leq 3. Three gentle steps to forget one qubit, where one classical bit allowed only the abrupt single step. The room to subdivide erasure grew, and it grew quadratically.

One honest caveat before we move on. That d^2 - 1 is an upper bound, and it came from a structural argument that does not, by itself, build anything. Whether some channel actually attains it, erasing in exactly d^2 - 1 steps and no fewer, is a separate and harder question. It is also the question the rest of this post is really about. We will approach it from an unlikely direction: not by ransacking the enormous space of all channels, but by tying our hands almost completely and seeing how close we can still get.

Clifford channels as the playground

We promised to tie our hands. The way to do it is to stop treating channels as arbitrary maps and start viewing them through one particular, highly structured basis of operations. Once the basis is in place, the channels we want will almost name themselves.

Begin, as always, with the qubit, where the basis is already famous. Every 2 \times 2 matrix is a combination of four: the identity I and the three Pauli matrices X, Y, Z. Here X is a bit flip, swapping |0\rangle and |1\rangle; Z is a phase flip; and Y is what you get by doing both. Because these four span every 2 \times 2 matrix, a qubit channel is fixed entirely by what it does to them. The Paulis are a coordinate system for quantum operations.

The same coordinate system exists in every dimension, and it grows out of two basic moves. Fix d levels, |0\rangle through |d-1\rangle. The first move is a shift: bump every level up by one, |j\rangle \to |j+1\rangle, wrapping around at the top. The second is a phase kick: multiply level j by a root of unity that depends on j. The shift generalises the bit flip X, the phase kick generalises Z.

Combine these two moves in all possible amounts and you get d^2 operators, one for each pair of exponents. We label them by that pair. Write \eta = (a, b), with a counting shifts and b counting phase kicks, and call the resulting operator \mathbf{w}(\eta), a Weyl operator. There are exactly d^2 of them, and like the four Paulis they form a basis: every d \times d matrix is some combination of Weyl operators. This is the coordinate system we will use for the rest of the post.

From here on we take d to be a prime. The reason is in the aside, but the short version is that the arguments ahead need to divide by the labels, not merely add them, and division behaves only when d is prime. The labels then live in a genuine two-dimensional plane, \mathbb{F}_d^2.

The operators explicitly, and why $d$ must be prime

Write \omega = e^{2\pi i / d}. The two moves are the shift and clock operators, X|j\rangle = |j + 1 \bmod d\rangle, \qquad Z|j\rangle = \omega^{\,j}|j\rangle, and a general Weyl operator is a product \mathbf{w}(a,b) \sim X^a Z^b, fixed up to a phase convention we will not need. The exponents a and b live modulo d: shift d times and you are back where you started.

In the bookkeeping ahead we will need to divide by these exponents, not just add and multiply them, and division modulo d works cleanly only when d is prime. So we take d prime. The integers modulo a prime form a field, written \mathbb{F}_d: a number system with addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division all behaving as you expect, just with finitely many elements. The labels then live in the plane \mathbb{F}_d^2, and h below will be ordinary linear algebra over that field.

One feature lifts the Weyl operators above being merely a basis. Multiply two of them and you get a third, up to a phase: \mathbf{w}(\eta)\,\mathbf{w}(\xi) = (\text{phase}) \cdot \mathbf{w}(\eta + \xi). The labels simply add. The leftover phase is the toll for working with matrices rather than plain vectors, and it is why this assignment is called a projective representation of the group of labels. We will never need the phase’s exact form, only that the labels add.

One label stands apart. The label \eta = 0 is the identity operator itself, the “trace direction” that every copier we allow has to leave untouched. The other d^2 - 1 labels are the directions that can actually be forgotten. That number should look familiar: it is exactly the d^2 - 1 dimensions of the quantum state space we counted on the Bloch ball. The Weyl labels are those dimensions, relabelled.

Now we can say which channels are tame. Apply a general channel to a Weyl operator and you get back some sprawling mixture of all d^2 of them. A Clifford channel refuses the sprawl. It sends each Weyl operator to a single other Weyl operator, rescaled: T[\mathbf{w}(\eta)] = \lambda(\eta)\,\mathbf{w}(h(\eta)). Two pieces of data, and nothing else.

The first piece is h. It says where each label goes: the operator labelled \eta lands on the one labelled h(\eta). This h is a linear map on the label plane, an invertible 2 \times 2 matrix over \mathbb{F}_d, an element of GL(2, \mathbb{F}_d). All it does is shuffle the coordinate system among itself.

The second piece is \lambda. It pins a number \lambda(\eta) to each label, the factor by which that coordinate is stretched or shrunk. This is where forgetting will live. A \lambda smaller than one in magnitude damps a coordinate; a \lambda equal to zero erases it outright.

The name is borrowed. The Clifford group is the set of quantum gates that map Paulis to Paulis under conjugation, the well-behaved gates at the centre of quantum error correction. Clifford channels are their noisy, irreversible cousins: maps that still respect the Weyl coordinate system even as they leak and forget.

It is worth dwelling on how little data this is. A general channel in dimension d is described by its Choi matrix, an object of size d^2 \times d^2 carrying on the order of d^4 free numbers. A Clifford channel is a 2 \times 2 matrix over \mathbb{F}_d together with a list of d^2 numbers. We have thrown away almost all the freedom. The whole wager of this post is that what survives is still enough.

And here is the move that makes the wager pay. We want to know what a copier does when it runs many times over, which means iterating T. For a general channel, iteration is a fog: mixtures of mixtures, with no visible structure to grab. For a Clifford channel, watch what happens to a single Weyl operator when we apply T again, and again. That is the next section.

The reduction (the pivot)

We left off promising to iterate. So let us iterate, and watch.

Apply T to a single Weyl operator. By definition it comes back rescaled and relabelled: T[\mathbf{w}(\eta)] = \lambda(\eta)\,\mathbf{w}(h(\eta)). Now apply T again. The output is itself a Weyl operator, so T knows exactly what to do with it: T^2[\mathbf{w}(\eta)] = \lambda(\eta)\,T[\mathbf{w}(h(\eta))] = \lambda(\eta)\,\lambda(h(\eta))\,\mathbf{w}(h^2(\eta)). Apply it n times and the pattern is plain: T^n[\mathbf{w}(\eta)] = \Biggl(\prod_{j=0}^{n-1} \lambda\bigl(h^j(\eta)\bigr)\Biggr)\,\mathbf{w}\bigl(h^n(\eta)\bigr).

Sit with that for a moment, because the fog has lifted completely.

Iterating the channel did two clean, separate things. The label moved, \eta \to h(\eta) \to h^2(\eta) \to \cdots, a single point hopping around the plane \mathbb{F}_d^2 under repeated application of the one matrix h. And an amplitude piled up, a running product of \lambda values, one factor collected at each label the point lands on.

That sequence of labels, \eta, h(\eta), h^2(\eta), \ldots, is the orbit of \eta under h. The entire life of the channel, run any number of times, is bookkept by two finite-field facts: how the orbit travels, and what \lambda does along it. The infinite-dimensional machinery of quantum channels has collapsed into a point hopping around a plane of d^2 labels, multiplying numbers as it goes.

Now recall what we are chasing. The completely depolarising channel P erases everything: it leaves the identity \mathbf{w}(0) alone and annihilates every other Weyl operator, P[\mathbf{w}(\eta)] = 0 \quad \text{for every } \eta \neq 0. That is just “every state goes to the maximally mixed state,” written in the Weyl coordinate system. The identity label takes care of itself: \lambda(0) = 1 because the channel preserves the trace, so \mathbf{w}(0) is fixed at every step. All the action is on the d^2 - 1 nonzero labels.

So asking for T^k = P is asking for the accumulated amplitude to be zero on every nonzero label after exactly k steps: \prod_{j=0}^{k-1} \lambda\bigl(h^j(\eta)\bigr) = 0 \qquad \text{for every } \eta \neq 0. A product of numbers is zero exactly when one of its factors is. So the condition says something you can picture: travelling along the orbit of any nonzero \eta, within the first k steps the point must pass through at least one label where \lambda vanishes. And k has to be the smallest such number, since we want the erasure to land at step k and not a step sooner. Push k as high as it will go while still erasing every orbit, and you have found the longest root.

This is the pivot of the whole post, so let me state it with no quantum mechanics in the sentence at all. We want a point that, started anywhere nonzero and moved repeatedly by h, takes as long as possible to first land on a label where \lambda vanishes. Two things govern that delay, and they are independent:

  1. How long the orbits are. If h carries a point through many distinct labels before it repeats, there is more room to postpone the first vanishing. This is a question about the matrix h by itself: its order, and how it cuts the d^2 - 1 nonzero labels into orbits. That is the next section.

  2. Where \lambda is allowed to vanish. We need \lambda to hit zero at least once on every orbit, but as late as possible. And \lambda is not free to vanish wherever it pleases: a constraint inherited from complete positivity forces its zeros to come in pairs, and that pairing is exactly what costs us a factor of two. That is the section after.

Solve those two and the bound drops out. The astonishing part, already half in view, is that neither question mentions a quantum channel any more. From here on we are doing linear algebra over a finite field.

Ingredient 1: the order of h

The first ingredient is the easy one to state. We want the point to visit as many labels as possible before it starts repeating, so we want the orbits of h to be long. How long can they get?

There are d^2 - 1 nonzero labels in all, so no orbit can be longer than that. The happy fact is that the maximum is reached. There exist matrices h in GL(2, \mathbb{F}_d) whose single orbit sweeps through every nonzero label before returning home: one grand cycle of length d^2 - 1, visiting all of \mathbb{F}_d^2 \setminus \{0\}. Such an h acts transitively on the nonzero labels.

Where do these maximal matrices come from? From a second field hiding inside the first. The plane \mathbb{F}_d^2 is two-dimensional over \mathbb{F}_d, and it happens to also be a larger field, the field \mathbb{F}_{d^2} with d^2 elements. In any finite field the nonzero elements, multiplied together, form a single cycle: there is a generator whose powers run through every nonzero element exactly once. Multiplication by that generator is an \mathbb{F}_d-linear map on the plane, and as a matrix it is our maximal h.

Primitive polynomials, and why the order is exactly $d^2 - 1$

A primitive polynomial of degree two over \mathbb{F}_d is an irreducible quadratic whose root generates the multiplicative group of \mathbb{F}_{d^2}. Call that root g. Its powers g, g^2, g^3, \ldots cycle through all d^2 - 1 nonzero elements of \mathbb{F}_{d^2} before g^{\,d^2 - 1} = 1 brings them back, so g has multiplicative order exactly d^2 - 1.

Viewing \mathbb{F}_{d^2} as the plane \mathbb{F}_d^2, multiplication by g is a linear map, and in a basis it is the companion matrix of the primitive polynomial. It inherits the order d^2 - 1, and that is the largest order any element of GL(2, \mathbb{F}_d) can have. (Every other kind of element, the diagonalisable ones and the single-eigenvalue ones, tops out lower; the irreducible “Singer” elements like this one are the champions.)

It pays to see one. Take d = 3, so we are working modulo 3 and the plane has 3^2 - 1 = 8 nonzero points. The polynomial x^2 + x + 2 is primitive over \mathbb{F}_3, and its companion matrix is h = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}. Start at the label (1,0) and apply h over and over, doing all the arithmetic modulo 3: (1,0) \to (0,1) \to (1,2) \to (2,2) \to (2,0) \to (0,2) \to (2,1) \to (1,1) \to (1,0). Eight steps, eight distinct labels, every nonzero point of the plane visited exactly once, and then home. This h has order 8 = 3^2 - 1, the most a 2 \times 2 matrix over \mathbb{F}_3 can manage. One orbit, as long as the plane allows.

If \lambda could vanish at just a single point of this cycle, we would be done, and we would reach the full quantum bound: a point starting one step past the lone zero would have to travel all d^2 - 1 steps to come back to it. That would be a root of order d^2 - 1. But \lambda cannot vanish at only one point, and the reason is the second ingredient.

Ingredient 2: orbits and where \lambda vanishes

So we need \lambda to vanish somewhere on the orbit, and we would like it to vanish as rarely as possible, ideally just once. Here the channel pushes back. The amplitudes \lambda are not ours to set freely, and the constraint comes in two layers.

The first layer is a symmetry. A quantum channel T sends Hermitian operators to Hermitian operators, since the density matrices it acts on are Hermitian. Tracing what that requires of the amplitudes gives a clean relation between the label \eta and its negative: \lambda(-\eta) = \overline{\lambda(\eta)}. Take absolute values and the consequence is stark: |\lambda(-\eta)| = |\lambda(\eta)|. The amplitude at a label and the amplitude at its mirror image always have the same size. In particular, if \lambda vanishes at \eta, it must vanish at -\eta as well. The zeros come in mirror pairs.

The second layer is stronger, and we will lean on it shortly. A channel is not merely required to preserve Hermiticity; it must be completely positive. That places a further positivity condition on a matrix built from the values of \lambda, one the mirror-pair symmetry alone does not guarantee. We will come back to it once we have seen what the mirror pairing already forces.

The Hermiticity argument

This relation comes from T preserving Hermiticity, a property shared by every quantum channel but in itself weaker than full complete positivity. A Weyl operator is not Hermitian, but its adjoint is another Weyl operator: \mathbf{w}(\eta)^\dagger = \mathbf{w}(-\eta), up to the same phase convention we have been ignoring. A channel obeys T[A^\dagger] = T[A]^\dagger, because it must turn Hermitian inputs into Hermitian outputs. Apply this to A = \mathbf{w}(\eta). On one side, T[\mathbf{w}(\eta)^\dagger] = T[\mathbf{w}(-\eta)] = \lambda(-\eta)\,\mathbf{w}(h(-\eta)). On the other, T[\mathbf{w}(\eta)]^\dagger = \overline{\lambda(\eta)}\,\mathbf{w}(h(\eta))^\dagger = \overline{\lambda(\eta)}\,\mathbf{w}(-h(\eta)). Since h is linear, h(-\eta) = -h(\eta), so the two Weyl operators match and the coefficients must agree: \lambda(-\eta) = \overline{\lambda(\eta)}.

Now put the two ingredients together, and here is something worth pausing on. The mirror-pair constraint pairs every nonzero label \eta with its negative -\eta, and for odd prime d those are always distinct points of the plane. The grand cycle has length d^2 - 1, an even number, and the mirror map \eta \mapsto -\eta sends you exactly halfway around it. The two paired zeros sit at opposite poles of the cycle, cutting it into two equal arcs of length (d^2 - 1)/2; Figure 4 shows the d = 3 case.

What about d = 2? At d = 2 arithmetic is modulo 2, so -1 = 1, and every label is its own mirror. The pairing constraint collapses: it no longer forces a second zero anywhere. A single vanishing of \lambda on the three-element cycle is enough, and the longest possible delay is the full cycle length, 3. That is exactly the general quantum bound d^2 - 1 = 3.

So the qubit is not an awkward edge case. It is the one case where Clifford channels already saturate the full quantum bound, because the symmetry tax that costs a factor of 2 for larger d does not apply at d = 2. The factor-of-two gap between Clifford and general opens up at d = 3 and stays open from then on.

And that settles it. Whichever point you start from, you reach one of the two zeros within half a turn of the cycle. The longest you can be made to wait is the length of one arc, (d^2 - 1)/2 steps.

One technical point remains, and I would rather flag it than slip past. Choosing where \lambda vanishes is not the same as proving that such a \lambda comes from a genuinely completely positive channel. This is the second layer from a moment ago: complete positivity asks a certain matrix assembled from the values of \lambda to be positive semidefinite, and the mirror pairing alone does not deliver it. It can be met, though. Keep the nonzero amplitudes modest away from the paired zeros and the channel stays completely positive, so suitable \lambda do exist; we simply do not get free run of the whole half-cycle. The thesis works the positivity criterion out in full (§5.2.1).

With that secured, we have built a Clifford channel that erases in exactly k = \frac{d^2 - 1}{2} steps and no fewer. A Clifford root of the completely depolarising channel of that order exists.

For our qutrit, d = 3, that is a root of order 4: four gentle steps to erase a qutrit using nothing but the two-piece Clifford data. The mirror pair of zeros sits at (1,0) and (2,0) in the cycle above, and every other label is at most four steps from one of them.

Punchline

Line up the three bounds. How finely can you divide total erasure, the completely depolarising channel, into equal steps?

  • Classically, with a bistochastic copier: d - 1 steps.
  • With a general quantum channel: d^2 - 1 steps.
  • With a Clifford channel: d^2 - 1 at d = 2, dropping to \dfrac{d^2 - 1}{2} for d an odd prime.

For a qubit (d = 2): 1 classically, 3 generally, 3 with Clifford channels. The qubit is the case where the Clifford toolbox is already the full toolbox.

For a qutrit (d = 3): 2 classically, 8 generally, 4 with Clifford channels. Here the gap opens. The Clifford copier, carrying almost no information about itself, lands at 4: within a factor of two of the full quantum power of 8, and already twice the classical 2.

That is the part worth carrying away. When we restricted from all quantum channels down to Clifford channels, we threw away almost all the freedom. A general channel needs of order d^4 numbers to describe; a Clifford channel needs a 2 \times 2 matrix over \mathbb{F}_d and a short list of amplitudes. You might have guessed the bound would collapse most of the way back to the classical d - 1. It barely moved.

The reason is that the room to subdivide erasure never lived in the richness of the channel in the first place. It lived in the orbit structure of a single linear map on a small finite plane. The longest root is as long as the longest orbit of h, and a well-chosen h runs through all d^2 - 1 labels at once. The only thing the quantum machinery buys you over this finite-field skeleton is the factor of two, and even that is not channel richness: it is the one symmetry, \lambda(-\eta) = \overline{\lambda(\eta)}, that Hermiticity preservation stamps on the amplitudes and nothing more.

A question that began with photocopiers and density matrices turns out to be answered, almost entirely, by asking how long a point can wander in the plane \mathbb{F}_d^2 before it is forced to step on a marked square. That is the unreasonable beauty promised at the top.

What’s next

One loose thread remains: the factor of two. Is (d^2 - 1)/2 the end of the story, or just the end of the Clifford story? It is only the Clifford story. The full quantum bound d^2 - 1 really can be reached, by a channel that gives up the tidy Weyl structure we leaned on. Instead of asking a single matrix h to do all the work, one builds the root directly at the level of the channel’s representation, nudging a Jordan block into exactly the shape the bound allows. That construction does not care about mirror pairs, so the factor of two disappears, and the gap to d^2 - 1 closes.3

That more general result subsumes the Clifford one, which is why the Clifford construction does not appear in the final paper. I wanted to write it up anyway, because it is the cleanest place I know to actually see the finite-field representation theory at work. In the general construction the orbit structure is buried inside linear algebra over \mathbb{C}. Here it sits on the surface: a point hopping around a plane of d^2 labels, a matrix of order d^2 - 1, and a single symmetry that costs a factor of two. The whole argument fits in the palm of your hand, and you can check the d = 3 case by stepping around an eight-point cycle with a pencil.

The full development, including the classical and general quantum bounds and the Clifford channels above, is in my diploma thesis.4
